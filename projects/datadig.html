My part was making that grouping stable over time, so a story keeps its identity as new articles arrive. meta: When: 2025–2026 Role: Clustering, story chains and retrieval, in a team system Code: Not public ---

Why identity matters

Every few minutes, new snippets are extracted from incoming articles and the recent ones are clustered again. Timelines, summaries and alerts all point at cluster IDs. If a re-run splits yesterday’s cluster in two, or gives it a new ID, the story’s history is cut in half even though nothing about the story changed.

Hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage does exactly this on streaming data. Its merges are greedy, so adding a few points changes the merge order, and in our runs about 1% of the members of existing clusters ended up scattered across new ones.

Anti-split, anti-merge

After each re-clustering, a repair pass runs in two phases. In the first, the members of each old cluster that were scattered across new labels go back to the label that holds most of them. In the second, any new label that swallowed several old clusters is split back into one label per old cluster. Ties are broken by the smallest label, so the pass is deterministic.

New clusters then inherit old IDs by Jaccard overlap of their members. One more detail mattered in practice: the language model rewrites a snippet’s wording slightly on each pass, which moves its embedding and can ripple through the clustering. When the new embedding is within cosine 0.9 of the old one, the old vector is kept.

Story chains

Clusters are linked into chains that follow a story over days and weeks. Each node in a chain stores only what changed since the previous one, not a fresh summary of everything, so a reader can see how a story moved instead of rereading it.

For retrieval I used a grounded variant of HyDE: look up real documents first, skip generation entirely if the evidence is weak, and only then let the model write a short bridging query from what was found. A hypothetical document written from nothing tends to retrieve the model’s own assumptions.

Scale

In an offline replay of three days of news on an earlier version of the pipeline, 29,832 extracted snippets came down to 7,628 after de-duplication and to 4,510 clusters, which the model then merged into existing stories, opened as new ones, or dropped as noise.
